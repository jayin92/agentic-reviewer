# ICLR 2027 reviewer profile

> **Which edition, and where the form comes from.** The ICLR 2027 Reviewer Guide, AI Policy for Reviewers, and Author Guidelines are published on iclr.cc, so this file covers ICLR 2027. The 2027 review form is **not public yet**. Reviewing runs Oct 1 to Oct 21, 2026, reviews come out Nov 5, 2026, and the OpenReview invitation `ICLR.cc/2027/Conference/-/Official_Review` returned "not found" on 2026-09-26. The **Review form** and **Score meanings** sections therefore copy the **official ICLR 2026 form**, taken from the OpenReview invitation that defines it. Every row is marked *2027: unconfirmed*. Public ICLR 2026 review pages on openreview.net sat behind a browser-verification challenge, so I couldn't read them directly. The invitation schema is the form definition those pages render.

## What reviewers are asked to evaluate
- Decide "whether a submission will bring sufficient value to the community and contribute new knowledge" (2027 Reviewer Guide).
- **Be rigorous.** Read the paper carefully and check the arguments in enough detail to be convinced of the main claims. Place the paper in its broader literature.
- **Be open-minded.** Not every paper has to compete on an established leaderboard or sit in an established theoretical framework. Ask whether the results are likely to interest or surprise a broad audience, or could take the field in new directions. Be mindful of your own biases and judge value to the whole ICLR community, not just to you.
- **Be concise (new emphasis in 2027).** Longer reviews aren't always better. Raise only points likely to affect your final accept/reject decision.
- While reading, identify the objective of the work: a known problem, a new problem or application, a new theoretical finding, or a mix. Then assess its strengths and weaknesses on clarity, technical correctness, experimental rigor, reproducibility, and novelty (theoretical, algorithmic, etc.).
- Answer four key questions:
  1. What specific question or problem does the paper tackle?
  2. Is the approach well motivated and well placed in the literature?
  3. Does the paper support its claims? Are the theoretical or empirical results correct and scientifically rigorous?
  4. How significant is the work? Does it add new knowledge and enough value for the community? This doesn't require state-of-the-art results.
- Structure the review in this order:
  1. Summary of the claimed contributions, written positively and constructively.
  2. Strong and weak points, as complete as possible.
  3. Initial recommendation (accept or reject) with one or two key reasons.
  4. Supporting arguments.
  5. Questions for the authors.
  6. Extra feedback to improve the paper, clearly marked as not part of the decision.
- Complete the Code of Ethics (CoE) report. It asks two questions: is there a potential violation, and if so, why.
- Engage in the author discussion and stay open to changing your rating in either direction. Attend the borderline-paper meeting if the AC calls one. Give a final recommendation that says what changed your view during discussion and what didn't.

## What reviewers are told NOT to do
- Don't reject a paper for lacking state-of-the-art results alone: "a lack of state-of-the-art results does not by itself constitute grounds for rejection."
- Don't reject a paper for missing comparisons to contemporaneous work or to arXiv-only papers: "the lack of such comparisons cannot be a basis for rejection." You may point such work out to the authors.
- Don't request experiments that would significantly change the paper. Requested experiments "should be limited in scope and serve to more thoroughly validate existing results from the submission."
- Don't pad the review. "Pointing out typos, or suggesting peripheral citations and supplementary experiments just add to everyone's workload without changing the final conference program." The guide warns specifically that LMs make it easy to produce exhaustive reviews with dozens of points.
- Don't rely on the arXiv version: "It is recommended that you ignore the version on arxiv."
- Supplementary material and appendices are optional reading. Reviewers "are not required to read the appendix", but should consider checking the supplement before asking the authors a question.
- Don't search for author identities. For example, if a paper reports a challenge ranking, "reviewers will be advised to not intentionally search the authors by examining the leaderboard" (Author Guidelines FAQ).
- Don't use AI undisclosed or irresponsibly. Don't use AI to fill in the initial self-report. Don't submit LLM hallucinations or falsehoods (see the LLM policy below).
- Don't submit late or low-quality reviews. Late reviewers, and their co-authors, can't see the reviews of their own papers until they finish. Reviewers who ignore AC warnings about low-quality reviews may have their own papers desk-rejected.
- Don't raise non-CoE policy violations such as plagiarism, double submission, length, or formatting in the review text. Send them to the AC/SAC/PC as a confidential comment. Flag CoE violations through the CoE report.

## Concurrent-work policy
From the 2027 Reviewer Guide FAQ: "We consider papers contemporaneous if they are published within the last two months. That means, since our full paper deadline is September 16, if a paper was published (i.e., at a peer-reviewed venue) on or after **July 17, 2026**, authors are not required to compare their own work to that paper."
- arXiv doesn't count as a peer-reviewed venue. Authors aren't required to compare to papers that appear only on arXiv.
- Authors are "strongly encouraged to cite" contemporaneous or arXiv work they know about. Reviewers may point such work out, but missing comparisons "cannot be a basis for rejection."
- **Inconsistency, unconfirmed.** The guide names September 16 as the full-paper deadline. The 2027 Call for Papers and Author Guidelines give **Sep 25, 2026 11:59 PM AOE**. Two months before Sep 25 would be about July 25, 2026. The published cutoff is **July 17, 2026**, and I couldn't confirm whether it will be corrected. The guide's text looks carried over from 2026: the 2026 guide said "September 24" and "July 24, 2025".

## Score meanings
All labels are verbatim from the ICLR 2026 form. None are confirmed for 2027.

**Soundness / Presentation / Contribution** (same scale for all three)
- 4: excellent
- 3: good
- 2: fair
- 1: poor

**Rating** (ICLR 2026 allowed set: 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10; the 2027 set is unconfirmed)
- 0: strong reject.
- 2: reject, not good enough
- 4: marginally below the acceptance threshold. But would not mind if paper is accepted
- 6: marginally above the acceptance threshold. But would not mind if paper is rejected
- 8: accept, good paper (poster)
- 10: strong accept, should be highlighted at the conference as spotlight or oral.

**Confidence**
- 1: You are unable to assess this paper and have alerted the ACs to seek an opinion from different reviewers.
- 2: You are willing to defend your assessment, but it is quite likely that you did not understand the central parts of the submission or that you are unfamiliar with some pieces of related work. Math/other details were not carefully checked.
- 3: You are fairly confident in your assessment. It is possible that you did not understand some parts of the submission or that you are unfamiliar with some pieces of related work. Math/other details were not carefully checked.
- 4: You are confident in your assessment, but not absolutely certain. It is unlikely, but not impossible, that you did not understand some parts of the submission or that you are unfamiliar with some pieces of related work.
- 5: You are absolutely certain about your assessment. You are very familiar with the related work and checked the math/other details carefully.

## Other policies relevant to feedback
- **LLM use by reviewers (2027 AI Policy for Reviewers).**
  - "Limited and responsible use of AI tools to assist human writing of peer reviews" is permitted. Disclosure is mandatory.
  - If LLMs generated or edited any part of the review, the reviewer must submit the original self-written assessment and all LM interactions, across all turns. The self-report isn't shown to authors, but SACs and PCs may use it.
  - The following count as misrepresentation and are subject to discipline: LLM-generated falsehoods, hallucinations, or misrepresentations; low-quality reviews; using AI to fill in the self-report; and substantial mismatches between the self-report and the final review.
  - ICLR may use AI-detection tools. Penalties can include desk-rejecting all papers the reviewer authored and restrictions on future submissions.
  - The policy notes: "consider skipping the LLM and submitting your original text directly!"
  - For comparison, ICLR 2026 allowed LLMs "as a general-purpose writing assistance tool" and required disclosure. The 2026 guide said the form would include a field for this, but the ICLR 2026 Official_Review invitation I retrieved has no such field. How 2026 collected the disclosure is unconfirmed.
- **Author AI-use disclosure (2027).**
  - Papers must include a mandatory AI-use statement section, which doesn't count toward the page limit.
  - The AI Policy for Authors lists which uses must be disclosed and which are only recommended for disclosure.
  - A substantial LLM-produced falsehood, plagiarism, or misrepresentation is a CoE violation by the authors and may lead to desk rejection.
  - In 2026, reviewers who found undisclosed significant LLM use were told to notify the AC, and the 2026 form had an `LLM_usage` flag for this. Whether the flag stays in 2027 is unconfirmed.
- **Page limits.** Main text is 9 pages or fewer at submission and 10 pages during rebuttal and for camera-ready, strictly enforced. References and appendices are unlimited. Reviewers aren't required to read appendices.
- **Supplementary material.** Code is encouraged. Reviewers are "encouraged, but not required" to review supplementary material.
- **Recommended statements.**
  - An Ethics Statement (optional, at most 1 page, not counted in the page limit).
  - A paragraph-long Reproducibility Statement (optional, not counted), pointing to where reproducibility details live.
  - Missing optional statements aren't a stated rejection criterion.
- **Code of Ethics / Code of Conduct.** Reviewers must follow both (https://iclr.cc/public/CodeOfEthics, https://iclr.cc/public/CodeOfConduct). CoE concerns go in the CoE report at review time; the 2027 deadline for flagging is Nov 18.
- **Anonymity and arXiv.** Reviewing is double-blind. arXiv preprints are allowed and don't violate anonymity. Authors cite their own related arXiv work in the third person.
- **Revisions during discussion.**
  - Authors may revise the paper until Nov 18, 2026 (10-page limit), and a pdfdiff is provided.
  - "Area chairs and reviewers reserve the right to ignore changes that are significantly different from the original paper."
  - Reviewers aren't required to read every revision.
- **Openness.** Reviews are anonymous but public on OpenReview. All submissions, including rejected and withdrawn ones, are de-anonymized and released after decisions. The review text should assume a public audience.
- **Additional experiments.** Allowed only when limited in scope and aimed at validating existing results.
- **2027 timeline (Reviewer Guide, "subject to minor changes").**
  - Bidding: Sep 18 to Sep 25.
  - Reviews due: Oct 1 to Oct 21.
  - Reviews released: Nov 5.
  - Author discussion: Nov 5 to Nov 18.
  - Reviewer/AC discussion: Nov 19 to Nov 25.
  - Final recommendation due: Nov 25.
  - Borderline meetings: Nov 26 to Dec 2.
  - Decisions: Dec 16 (Author Guidelines).
- **Reciprocal reviewing (2027).** Authors on 3 or more papers must review at least 6 papers. Every submission needs at least one qualified author registered to review at least 3 papers. Incomplete or low-quality reviews by the rebuttal stage can lead to desk rejection of that reviewer's own papers.
